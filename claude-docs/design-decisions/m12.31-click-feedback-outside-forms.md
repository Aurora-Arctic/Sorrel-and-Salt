# M12.31 — Feedback from a click outside a form

**Status:** recommended, **not decided** · **Date:** 2026-10-19

The owner dislikes where Add to Cupboard shows its failure: to the right of the
button (`CupboardActions`, `src/components/IngredientEntry/cupboard-actions.tsx`).
That raised a wider question: when a person presses a button that sits outside
any form, how should the page say what happened, whether it worked or not? This
record scopes the question, inventories the controls it covers, sets out what
the research says, describes five designs, and recommends one. **The owner has
not yet chosen.** Section 5 is a recommendation, and nothing in the code, the
other docs or the task list is changed to match it until the owner decides.

The first version of the task asked only about errors. The owner's review widened
it to all feedback, so the task's title and entry now say so
([`tasks/m12.md`](../tasks/m12.md), M12.31).

An interactive comparison of the five designs, with the four screens and a
screen reader log, is published as a private page:
<https://claude.ai/artifact/R9BzSPjPbhKbvhbyLKREhm>. The footnote labels in this
record are that page's source numbers.

## 1. Scope and exceptions

The question covers a **click control that is not inside a form**: a button, a
menu item, or a row action that sends a request when pressed (or, for the
confirmed actions, opens a question that sends one). The reasons for each edge:

- **Forms are out.** A form already has a place for its sentence about the
  whole view (a `Notice` for its root error and for its sent confirmation) and
  its own field errors, and its submit button is the one control that speaks
  there. [`notice.md`](../components/notice.md) describes that use. Nothing
  here changes it.
- **Modals and confirm overlays are out.** When the question, the confirm
  button and the failure are all inside the dialog, the person is already
  attending to that dialog, and it can carry its own alert. `OwnerExit` does
  this today: it holds its own `ActionFailure` inside the dialog
  (`LeaveWorkspaceButton/owner-exit.tsx`). The dialog is the place for the
  message.
- **A click that navigates is an exception to the success half.** Accept on an
  invitation takes the person to the coven; Sign Out and the sign-in buttons
  leave for another page. The new page is the confirmation, and the button
  stays busy until it arrives, so a success message would be said once and then
  discarded with the page. Such a control needs no success feedback. It does
  need failure feedback, because on a failure the person stays where they are.
  This follows the sources' advice to skip a success message when the interface
  already shows the result (Primer and GOV.UK both say so).[^8][^12]
- **A confirm modal followed by a failure on the page is borderline, and is
  treated as in scope for the message.** Revoke, Remove, Change Role, Leave
  Coven and the like open a modal; the confirm button in it sends the request.
  The confirm click is excluded as a click in a modal. But the failure always
  arrives after the modal has closed (`UserList/confirmed-action.tsx` sets the
  step back to idle and only then reports), so the sentence lands on the page,
  not in the dialog. The person is then looking at the row they acted on. So
  the **message** follows the same rule as a direct control, and its place is
  the open button's place. The **confirm click** and what happens inside the
  dialog stay out of scope.

Two limits on this record's reach: Better Auth controls are in scope even
though they never call `graphqlRequest` (section 2 explains), and nothing
here applies to a refusal that is not the result of a click, such as a page
that fails to load.

**Where the docs stand today.** `ActionFailure` says a refusal once, as an alert
above the controls it wraps; the latest report replaces the one before; outside a
wrapper its hook gives `null` and the control must say the refusal itself, so it

`warn` are `<p role="alert">`, `success` is `<output>` (a status), and `quiet`
turns an error or warning into a status. **No toast, snackbar or tip exists in
the app**, and neither component times out or has a way to dismiss. One
sentence in `notice.md` needs the owner's eye: it says a notice mounted with its
text is announced on arrival, while MDN says an alert inserted already populated
generally is not.[^3] Section 6 holds a task to test it.

## 3. The research

Done on 2026-10-19 from web pages only; nothing in the repository was involved.
The fetch tool returned a machine summary of each page, not the page itself, so
this section **paraphrases and quotes nothing**. Anyone who wants to rely on a
figure or a phrase should check it on the page. No source studies the exact case
here (a button or row action on a page, outside a form and a modal); where a
source is stretched to fit it, the text says so.

### What a screen reader hears

- WCAG success criterion 4.1.3 asks that changes such as the result of an
  action, a waiting state or the presence of errors reach assistive technology
  without moving focus. Its techniques pair `role="status"` with results and
  `role="alert"` with errors and warnings.[^1]
- A status is polite: it is read when the person is idle. An alert is assertive,
  and MDN asks that it be used sparingly, for what needs attention at once,
  because several at once make a bad experience.[^2][^3]
- The live region should already be in the page, empty, before its text
  changes, and the sources say an element inserted already carrying its message
  generally is not announced. WCAG's own technique for error alerts says the
  container must be in the page on load and clears it before refilling.[^3][^4][^5]
  The cost for this app is a region per row, present before use, and a row
  inserted later cannot carry its own fresh one.
- Do not give an alert both `role="alert"` and `aria-live`: MDN reports double
  speaking in VoiceOver on iOS. For a status it suggests adding a redundant
  `aria-live="polite"` for compatibility.[^4] Pickering also pairs
  `role="status"` with `aria-live="polite"`.[^6]
- A live region is read as plain text, so a link or a button inside it may not
  be conveyed. A Retry or Undo cannot rely on the region alone.[^4][^7]
- Primer's view: always announce the results of failed actions, and judge other
  changes case by case.[^8]

### Where it appears

- People tend to miss changes away from where they are looking, even when
  warned, and expect the result of an action near the action.[^9] A passive
  banner far from the person's focus can be missed.[^10] The NN/g error advice
  is to put the message near the error's source; it is written about forms, and
  applying it to row actions is our extension.[^11]
- Primer lists the weaknesses of toasts: they may sit outside the field of view
  or the magnified area, cover content including buttons in the bottom corners,
  and weaken the link between message and cause when far from it. Their place in
  the page's reading order may also be far from the trigger.[^8]
- GOV.UK puts its notification banner before the page heading and says to use it
  sparingly, for information not directly part of the task at hand; information
  that is part of the task belongs in the main content.[^12] A row action is
  part of the task, so this points away from a page-level banner for it.
- Primer's inline message sits near the action (beside a button, under an input,
  within a table) and is not page-level; Carbon puts inline notifications near
  the related item.[^13][^14] Primer's banner goes near the relevant section or
  above the page heading, one at a time.[^15] Salazar lists a persistent status
  region, inline feedback next to the trigger, and a dismissible alert area as
  alternatives to toasts.[^16]
- **No source says left, right or below the control.** Their rule is nearness
  and noticeability, so the side is a design choice and not a finding.[^9][^11]

### How long it stays

- WCAG 2.2.1 allows a message that disappears if the same information is
  available another way, and gives a new-email toast whose email still shows in
  the Inbox. Without that, the criterion applies to the message.[^17] The W3C
  discussion behind that text ended on the need for redundancy: the toast is a
  nice-to-have, never the only source.[^18] Criterion 2.2.2 may be read as
  covering messages that vanish on a timer; that is our reading of the summary,
  and the page does not name toasts.[^19]
- The ARIA Authoring Practices advise against alerts that disappear on their
  own, and ask designers to limit how often they appear.[^20] Criterion 2.2.4
  (Level AAA) asks that interruptions can be postponed, except emergencies;
  it supports keeping the loud channel for failure.[^21]
- NN/g notes that a validation message stays until the person acts, that a toast
  is a poor vehicle for an error, and gives a case of a person waiting five
  minutes after an error toast faded in five seconds.[^10]
- Carbon's inline notifications do not dismiss themselves, and users should be
  able to find a toast's information elsewhere after it goes. Red Hat's timed
  toasts clear after about eight seconds, and it advises a persistent one when
  the message matters; Singapore's GovTech defaults to five seconds for short,
  routine messages. **These are product defaults, not research.**[^14][^22][^23]
  Pickering is the exception, advising that notifications disappear on their own
  if the information remains elsewhere in the interface.[^6]
- Atlassian says always offer a way to dismiss; Primer's critical banners stay
  until resolved.[^24][^15] GOV.UK removes its success banner on the next page
  and says it needs more research on dismissal.[^12]
- **No source gives a researched time a message must stay up.**

### Success against failure

- For success, Primer says a simple action needs no second confirmation when
  the result is visible, and that an extra toast may reduce trust; its banner
  guidance says to use a success message only when the success is not easily
  seen.[^8][^15] GOV.UK is similar.[^12] In this app, a row that vanishes or a
  role word that changes is its own confirmation.
- Against that, WCAG technique G199 asks for consistent, explicit feedback that
  an action succeeded. A person using a screen reader cannot see the row
  vanish, so a polite status is how they get what a sighted person gets from
  the page.[^25]
- For failure, Primer offers a banner (available but passive) or a dialog
  (a deliberate interruption); NN/g, Red Hat and Singapore's system all say not
  to rely on a toast alone for an error that matters.[^8][^10][^22][^23]
  Messages should be plain, say what went wrong, and suggest a fix.[^11][^26]
  WCAG 3.3.1 wants an error indicated in text, with examples that are all
  forms.[^27]
- WCAG and MDN pair success with `status` and errors with `alert`.[^1][^2][^3]
  GOV.UK's success banner uses `role="alert"`, but that is a page-load case
  and not a click on a row.[^12]

### Focus

- A status message must not take focus; focus moving is a change of context,
  which the criterion excludes. MDN says the same for `alert` and `status`, and
  says that if focus has to move, a live region is the wrong tool. The
  Authoring Practices say an alert that must interrupt the person's work should
  be an alert dialog.[^1][^2][^3][^20]
- A control that triggers a message keeps its focus. **A row action that removes
  its own row loses its focus target**, so the page must say where focus lands.
  That follows from removing a focused element; no source tests it for row
  actions. WCAG 2.4.3 covers only content inserted after a trigger, and dialogs
  returning focus to their trigger, not notifications.[^28]
- A message holding a control (Retry, Dismiss, Undo) must be reachable by
  keyboard. A toast at the end of the page is hard to reach, and Escape can
  close the wrong one when several are open. Carbon's actionable notifications
  trap focus, which it calls disruptive, and it suggests a persistent area or a
  hotkey-reachable region.[^7][^8][^29] Material's Android snackbar allows one
  optional action.[^30]

### Small screens

- Primer says bottom-corner toasts can cover buttons and, at large text sizes,
  cover content or overflow; Roselli adds that a dismiss button needs a large
  enough touch target.[^7][^8]
- Tips that depend on hover do not exist on touch. NN/g recommends a tip opened
  by tapping an icon, and Pickering proposes a click-activated toggletip backed
  by an empty status region.[^31][^32] Content that appears on hover or focus
  must be dismissible, hoverable and persistent.[^33]
- A narrow row has less room beside a control, which argues for placing a
  message under the control or row rather than beside it. **That is our
  inference.** Material's snackbars can be anchored above another view, one at a
  time.[^30] No source tests row-level feedback on narrow screens.

### Where the sources disagree

- **Toasts at all.** Primer says they raise significant accessibility concerns
  and are not recommended; Roselli expects them to fail an audit; Silver, citing
  GitHub's testing, concludes that making them work for everyone is not
  possible.[^7][^8][^34] Carbon, Material, Red Hat and Singapore ship them,
  usually with a persist option and a rule that they are never the only
  channel.[^14][^22][^23][^30] The W3C thread did not settle it.[^18]
- **Disappearing on a timer.** Authoring Practices say avoid it; Pickering and
  the Understanding page say it can be fine when the information is elsewhere;
  Primer and Roselli say a timed toast violates 2.2.1.[^6][^7][^8][^17][^20]
- **Whether success needs saying.** G199 says yes; Primer and GOV.UK say skip it
  when the result shows.[^8][^12][^15][^25]
- **Alert or status for a positive message.** GOV.UK uses an alert; Red Hat uses
  a polite status; Singapore's toasts are assertive; WCAG and MDN say
  status.[^1][^2][^12][^22][^23]
- **How near an inline message sits.** Carbon and Primer's inline message say
  next to the item; the banners say section or page. None addresses a list of
  row controls.[^12][^13][^14][^15]

### Not reached or unverified

- **Material Design 3's snackbar page**: the fetch returned nothing. The
  Material Components for Android library doc was used instead, which is a
  library doc and not the design guidance. Snackbar durations and the M3 rule on
  errors are unverified.
- **Atlassian's flag component pages**: the fetch returned nothing. A search
  snippet reported an eight-second auto-dismiss; not read. Atlassian's success
  messages page was read and is cited.
- **The Scottish Government design system**: its reason for excluding toasts
  (that they might vanish before the person notices) comes from a search
  snippet only, as the page body came back empty.
- **GOV.UK on toasts**: no backlog item or discussion found. Whether it avoids
  toasts on purpose, and why, is not established.
- **Sheri Byrne-Haber's "Designing Toast Messages for Accessibility"**
  (Access \* Ability, 11 December 2023): paywalled after the opening, so her
  advice is unread. A search snippet credited a "five seconds plus one second
  per 120 characters" rule to a source not identified; it is unverified and not
  used.
- **Varakin, Levin and Fidler, "Unseen and Unaware: Implications of Recent
  Research on Failures of Visual Awareness for Human-Computer Interface Design"**
  (_Human-Computer Interaction_ 19, no. 4 [2004]: 389–422): the publisher page
  refused the fetch. Only the bibliographic record is known, not its findings.
  It is probably the most relevant academic source, and someone with access
  should read it.
- **Rensink, O'Regan and Clark, "To See or Not to See: The Need for Attention to
  Perceive Changes in Scenes"** (_Psychological Science_ 8, no. 5 [1997]): not
  read. Rensink's 2018 retrospective and NN/g's article on change blindness were
  read in its place.
- **Work on how long a message should stay or how long it takes to read**: none
  found. The durations in section 3 are product defaults.
- **Controlled studies of screen reader users missing live-region updates**:
  none found. That repeated identical messages may go unannounced is plausible
  but unverified; the only reports were two anecdotal write-ups seen as search
  snippets.
- **Adrian Roselli**: only "Defining 'Toast' Messages" was read. Primer's page
  is undated, and no Primer page was found that says toasts are deprecated;
  the page read says only that they are not recommended. (The design page's Design
  E card says Primer "deprecates" them, which overstates what was read.)

## 4. The five designs

The comparison page shows each on four screens: Add to Cupboard on a coven's
entry page, a members row's Change Role, an admin users row, and an invitation's
Accept and Decline. The letters match its cards.

- **A: Inline beside the control.** Today's `Notice` next to the pressed button,
  cleared on the next try. Success shows only the result. Near the action, no
  timer, and cheap. Weak points: its live region must be in the page before the
  text (see section 3), the side is a taste, and a success is silent for a
  screen reader.
- **B: One alert above the group.** Today's `ActionFailure`, widened to every
  screen: one alert above the controls, the latest report replacing the last.
  One region from page load is dependable for announcement, and it covers
  nothing. It sits away from the control and may be off screen on a long list.
- **C: Icon beside the control, message in a tip.** A warning or tick icon
  appears by the control and opens the message when pressed. It works for touch
  if opened by a press with an empty status region behind it. But tips are for
  help that is not essential, and a failure message is essential, so the page's
  version adds a short hidden alert; the evidence supports it only as a
  supplement to visible text.
- **D: Hybrid.** Success is the visible result plus a polite status that a
  screen reader announces. Failure is a dismissible alert in a space under the
  control that stays until dealt with. It follows the split most sources draw,
  and costs two behaviours to build and keep consistent. No source tests the
  pair.
- **E: Toast or snackbar.** A small message floats at the bottom and may fade
  (the page has a Timed Toasts switch to see both). It has the strongest
  evidence against it: Primer does not recommend it, and Roselli, Silver and the
  W3C thread agree on its timing and status-message risks. It appears away
  from the control and can cover content on a phone. If kept, it must persist,
  never be the only channel, and leave the result discoverable elsewhere.

## 5. Recommendation: Design D, the hybrid

**This section is the recommendation, not the decision. The owner has not yet
chosen.** If the owner accepts it, the rule is:

1. **Success is the visible result plus a polite `role="status"`.** If the page
   already shows the result (a row gone, a role word changed, a held mark), that
   is the confirmation for a sighted person. A single polite status region, in
   the page before any use, says the same in words for a screen reader, without
   moving focus. A click that navigates (section 1) says nothing.
2. **Failure is a persistent, dismissible `role="alert"` beside the control.** It
   has no timer: it stays until the person dismisses it, retries, or acts
   elsewhere. It does not move focus. Its dismiss button is reachable by
   keyboard. The alert carries the service's own sentence, as `ActionFailure`
   does today, and the control keeps its focus. "Beside" means in space the
   control owns, directly under it rather than to its right; the sources do not
   say which side (section 3), so this answers the owner's dislike of the
   right-hand side and nothing more.
3. **When a row removes itself, focus goes to the page heading.** A row that
   vanishes takes its focused button with it, and the sources are silent on
   where focus should land (section 3), so the rule has to say. The page
   heading is the landing M8.17 has chosen for a closed modal (its commit
   4b5cdb96 says focus goes back through the modal and route modal, with the
   page's heading last), so this rule should build on that work and needs it
   merged first.
4. **Modals keep their own alert inside.** A failure inside a dialog, as
   `OwnerExit` does today, stays there.

**Why D over the others.** It is the only design that gives a screen reader user
the explicit success G199 asks for and the plain failure channel every source
agrees a toast is a poor fit for, while leaving a sighted person's page as it is
for success. Against B it keeps the message at the control, which the change
blindness and proximity sources favour. Against A it adds the success status and
the dismiss. Against C it keeps the whole message as visible text. Against E it
avoids the one design the sources most often advise against.

**What D costs and leaves open.**

- It needs a mechanism for the status region and the per-control alert space,
  and every control to adopt it: the rows in section 2 differ today.
- On a phone's card rows the room under a control is narrow. `ActionFailure`
  places its alert above the list for exactly that reason, and nothing here
  refutes it. An open question for the owner: does D replace `ActionFailure`
  above a table, or sit beside it, with D's alert used for a control that
  stands alone? The recommendation is the second, since three screens use the
  wrapper already.
- It has not been tested with a screen reader. The sources are about toasts,
  banners and forms, not this case. The mount-time behaviour in section 2 is
  also unsettled.

**What becomes of Add to Cupboard's message.** Today it is the last child of the
flex row, to the right of the button, with no `ActionFailure` around it. Under D
the failure moves under the button, on its own line, as the persistent
dismissible alert. Its success is the page re-reading: the item is replaced by
Annotate, Make It Ours and Remove, and a held mark appears. The polite status
adds "added to your cupboard" in words. Not verified: whether the row survives the
re-read so its status region exists before use, and where focus is after the
menu closes.
