# Knotgrass

Common knotgrass, also called prostrate knotweed, is a low, mat-forming annual related to buckwheat and dock. Wikipedia says it grows in fields and wasteland across the temperate regions, apparently native to Eurasia, with green flowers edged white or pink from June to October. The name Polygonum comes from the Greek for many knees, for the prominent joints along the stem. Kynes records that in England a child who ate knotgrass was believed to stop growing, that a poppet stuffed with it was used to gain control of a person, and that the Anglo-Saxons called it unfortraedde, meaning untrodden to pieces. Cunningham calls it feminine, ruled by Saturn and earth, and holds some in the hand to bind woes: pour your problems into the herb, then burn it. He also says carrying it strengthens and protects the eyes. Kynes uses the stems in a banishing spell or to break a hex. Kynes's heading also covers equal-leaved knotgrass (P. arenastrum), so that citation is left for the curator.
