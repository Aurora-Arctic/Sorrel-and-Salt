# Grape

The common grape vine is a long-lived climbing shrub native to the Mediterranean, central Europe and south-western Asia, from Morocco and Portugal to southern Germany and northern Iran. It has a thick, woody base, deeply lobed leaves up to about twenty-three centimetres across and dense spikes of small greenish-white flowers, followed by fruit in hanging, tapering bunches. Grieve traces the word vine to a root meaning to twist, after its twining growth. It was domesticated thousands of years ago in south-western Asia, the Caucasus or around the Black Sea, and between five and ten thousand varieties are now grown on every continent but Antarctica. The fruit is eaten fresh, dried as raisins, or pressed for juice that ferments into wine and vinegar. Ancient peoples revered it: the Egyptians grew grapes by the third millennium BCE, painted them on tomb walls and buried wine with their great dead, and Babylonians, Phoenicians, Greeks and Romans offered wine to their gods as a means of communing with them. Magically it is a plant of fertility and plenty. The Romans painted grapes on garden walls to make the garden fruitful, and Cunningham holds that eating grapes or raisins aids fertility and strengthens the mind, and lays grapes on the altar during money spells. Kynes eats three grapes before divination or psychic work.
