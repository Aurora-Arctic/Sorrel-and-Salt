# Heather

Heather is a low evergreen shrub of the family Ericaceae, usually twenty to fifty centimetres tall, and the only species in its genus. It is native to Europe, Iceland, the Faroes and the Azores, has been carried to North America, Australia, New Zealand and the Falklands, and grows on open, acid ground. Its tiny scale-like leaves sit in opposite pairs, which tells it apart from the needle-leaved heaths, and from July to September it bears small flowers in clusters at the shoot tips, mauve in the wild and now and then white. The genus name probably comes from a Greek word meaning to beautify or sweep clean, after the besoms long made from it. Sheep, deer and grouse feed on it. People once dyed wool yellow and tanned leather with it, brewed heather beer with malt before hops arrived, and still prize heather honey. It is an emblem of Scotland and one of Norway's national flowers. White heather is lucky in Scotland, where it was thought to grow where faeries had rested, and another legend held that it stayed white because the blood of the Picts never stained it; in Ireland a sprig given was a declaration of love, and in Germany heather flowers were hung on sheds and barns at Walpurgis to keep witches from the cattle. Cunningham carries it as a guard against violent crime or for luck, burns it with fern out of doors to call rain, and notes its old use in conjuring ghosts.
