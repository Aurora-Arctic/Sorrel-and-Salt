# Heliotrope

European heliotrope is an annual herb of the borage family that grows from a taproot to about forty centimetres. Its stems carry soft grey-green hairs, its oval leaves are softly hairy too, and its small, fragrant white flowers, each with a yellow throat, line coiled spikes; the fruit is a bumpy nutlet. Native to Eurasia and North Africa, it has naturalised widely, in Australia and North America among other places, and grows as a roadside weed. Kynes sets it apart from the sweet-scented garden heliotrope, a different plant. The Anglo-Saxons used it against poisons and as a charm to settle unruly horses. Medieval magicians in France and Germany prized it, believing that, gathered at the right time, it could magnetise them and open esoteric knowledge, and a grimoire attributed to Albertus Magnus made an amulet of heliotrope and a wolf's tooth so that people would speak kindly of the wearer. Across medieval Europe and England it guarded against witches, demons and the possessed, and it was used to foretell storms. A sprig under the pillow was thought to bring dreams that showed where lost or stolen things lay; Cunningham says the thief himself will appear in the dream. He also puts it in exorcism incenses and healing sachets, carries it in a pocket or purse to draw money, and fills a small horn with it so that the wearer passes unnoticed.
