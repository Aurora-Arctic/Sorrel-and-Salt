# Greater Celandine

Greater celandine is an upright perennial of the poppy family, standing 30 to 120 cm tall, with blue-green, divided leaves whose lobes have wavy edges. From late spring through summer it carries small clusters of yellow, four-petalled flowers, followed by long, cylindrical capsules of small black seeds that ants carry away. A broken stem bleeds a yellow to orange latex, used in small amounts as a craft dye and an imitation gold paint, and a folk remedy for warts in France, the German-speaking countries, Hungary and Britain. Native to most of Europe and reaching into North Africa and western Asia, it grows on rocky slopes, in woodland, on waste ground and along roadsides, and in parts of North America it has become an aggressive invader. Despite the shared name it is not closely related to lesser celandine, which belongs to the buttercup family. Northcote passes on Gerard's account that the swallow healed its nestlings' eyes with the plant, which gave the genus its name of swallow-herb, and notes that it was once dedicated to the sun. Kynes tells of Anglo-Saxons tipping arrows with its sap to poison their quarry, and of German alchemists of the Middle Ages who called it caeli donum, a gift from heaven, said to grant foresight. Cunningham wears it against the skin, changed every three days, to escape entrapment and to lift the spirits, and wears it to court to win the favour of judge and jury.
