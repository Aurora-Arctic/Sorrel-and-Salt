# Gooseberry

The European gooseberry is a spreading, spiny shrub of the currant family, native to Europe, north-western Africa and south-western Asia and grown for its fruit. It reaches about a metre and a half, its branches set with sharp spines, its small leaves lobed, and its bell-shaped flowers hanging singly or in pairs; the berries, bristly or smooth, are most often green but may be red, purple, yellow or white. Where the "goose" in its name comes from is disputed, whether from a Dutch, German or French word or simply from goose and berry. Growers in Lancashire have aimed at size and those in the Lothians at flavour, and the fruit goes into tarts, pies, sauces, chutneys and jams, while the best wine from yellow gooseberries is said to resemble champagne. Since the Middle Ages it has also made a strong, sweet ale. Kynes gathers its lore: the Egyptian cat goddess was said to have gooseberry-green eyes; in Germany thorny branches were laid round cowsheds at Walpurgis and Beltane to keep witches out; faeries were thought fond of the fruit, hence the name fayberry; and children in Scotland and the north of England were warned off the bushes by tales of Awd Goggie, the faery who guards the unripe berries. Nineteenth-century slang gave the saying that babies are born under a gooseberry bush.
