# Miswak

Salvadora persica, called arak, toothbrush tree or mustard tree, is a small evergreen tree or shrub with a crooked trunk, native from the Middle East and North Africa to India. Its roots and twigs, called miswak, are traditionally chewed as a toothbrush: the outer bark is cut or peeled away to show inner fibres that act as bristles, and one stick can be trimmed again many times. The page says the use runs through the Arabian Peninsula, the Iranian Plateau and the wider Muslim world. The root bark has a pleasant fragrance of cress or mustard and a warm, pungent taste. The small red fruit is edible but pungent, the fresh leaves can be eaten in a salad, and the seeds can yield a detergent oil. The Miswak page says the World Health Organization recommended it in 1986, and that a 2000 consensus report called for more research.

Roster flag: the roster notes this is a tooth-cleaning stick, hygiene rather than ritual use. The Miswak page has a section "Religious prescriptions" saying hadith advise using it before wudu (ablution), before going to the mosque and at other times. The Miswak page names Maghrebi miswak as a different instrument, from walnut wood (Juglans regia). I recorded only Salvadora persica. The folk names are from the two pages as printed: the Miswak page lists "siwak" and "arak" among its names for the stick.
