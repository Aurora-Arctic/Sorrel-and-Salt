## What the Compendium Is

### Information, Not Advice

The compendium records what traditions, books and other sources say about ingredients: their correspondences, their folk names, the deities and festivals they are linked with, and what those sources say about handling them. It records these things so that a coven can look them up and cite them. It does not tell you what to do with them.

If you are ill, pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medicine or caring for someone who is, ask a doctor, pharmacist or other qualified professional before you eat, drink, burn, inhale or put on your skin anything described here. If you think you or someone else has been poisoned, contact your local poison centre or emergency service straight away.

## Safety

### Safety Notes Are Not Complete

A safety note on an ingredient is what a cited source said about it, as far as the curators have read. It is not a toxicology report. A note may cover one hazard and miss another; it may describe the ingredient as a whole and say nothing about a particular form; and many ingredients have no note at all because nobody has written one yet, not because the ingredient is safe.

So read every safety note as "at least this", never as "only this", and read the absence of a note as "nothing recorded", never as "nothing to worry about". The line under each ingredient's notes, and under each form's, says so and links here.

### A Familiar Ingredient Can Still Harm

An ingredient that is safe in its usual use can be harmful in another. Changing the form, the dose or the route into the body changes the risk. Two documented cases, both of everyday foods:

- **Bergamot.** An essence from the peel of this citrus fruit flavours Earl Grey tea, and its peel is sometimes candied. The same oil on the skin, followed by ultraviolet light, has caused blistering burns within two to three days, because of a compound in it called bergapten.[^1][^2]
- **Chilli.** Many people enjoy the heat chilli peppers add to food. Yet the compound that gives that heat, capsaicin, causes pain and redness when its oils get on the skin, intense pain if juice reaches an eye, and, when inhaled, can trigger an asthma attack or respiratory distress in susceptible people.[^3]

These are just some examples about how risk changes with use. Please do your own research and ask a doctor, pharmacist or other qualified professional for advice.
